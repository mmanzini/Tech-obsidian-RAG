# The Model Was Always the Point — MDD in the Age of AI

**Source:** [002-model-driven-development-in-the-age-of-ai.md](../../../Resources/Projects/articles-and-essays/002-model-driven-development-in-the-age-of-ai/002-model-driven-development-in-the-age-of-ai.md)
**Author:** Max (Massimiliano Alberto Manzini) — opinion essay, May 2026

---

## Summary

Max's opinion essay arguing that AI rehabilitates model-driven development by fixing the failure that sank it, while leaving intact the part worth keeping. The thesis: MDD's two translations — human-need → formal model, and model → running code — have *opposite* needs and want *opposite* tools. AI belongs in the upper translation (sense-making, where non-determinism is a feature) and must be kept out of the lower one in high-assurance domains (execution, where determinism is the asset). Companion take to the [[mdd-overview|MDD Standard]] reference; explicitly a personal position, not part of the Standard (source: 002-model-driven-development-in-the-age-of-ai.md).

## The promise that stalled

OMG's Model-Driven Architecture (2001) promised to make the model the primary artefact and code a generated by-product: a CIM for the domain, a PIM for structure, a PSM for implementation, with transformations carrying you down the stack (source: 002-model-driven-development-in-the-age-of-ai.md). It mostly didn't become the default. Two failures matter most for what comes next (source: 002-model-driven-development-in-the-age-of-ai.md):

- **The transformation barrier** — writing the rules that turned models into code (QVT, ATL) was itself specialist work. The abstraction meant to free you from low-level effort just relocated it.
- **Round-trip drift** — generate code, hand-edit it, and you have two sources of truth pulling apart. The "generate, don't hand-edit below the line" principle exists precisely because, in practice, people edited the code and the model rotted.

MDD survived where it could absorb these costs: long-lived enterprise systems, regulated domains, multi-platform targets. Low-code platforms (Mendix, OutSystems) are its most commercial descendants — and they succeeded by *hiding* the models from the people who didn't want to see them (source: 002-model-driven-development-in-the-age-of-ai.md).

## MDD is not SDD

A distinction the essay insists on: MDD makes a formal *model* (conforming to a metamodel) the contract and lets a *transformation engine* generate code; spec-driven development makes a structured *specification* the contract and lets humans or agents write the code. Today's tools — GitHub Spec Kit, AWS Kiro (prompt → EARS requirements → design → tasks) — are mostly SDD, the cousins of MDD, not MDD itself. The narrower, more interesting question: does AI make the *model-driven* approach more workable? (source: 002-model-driven-development-in-the-age-of-ai.md). See [[mdd-classic-vs-modern|Classic MDA vs Modern Practice]] for the spectrum.

## Four ways AI changes the maths

1. **Collapses the transformation barrier.** A deterministic tool needed a fully formal input; an LLM does not — it reads a PIM or half-formal description and produces platform code directly. The interpreter MDD spent two decades hand-building now exists as a general-purpose component (source: 002-model-driven-development-in-the-age-of-ai.md).
2. **Lowers the cost of the right language.** LLMs make drafting a grammar, metamodel and generator far less daunting, tilting the build-vs-reuse maths toward purpose-built DSLs that read naturally to domain experts (source: 002-model-driven-development-in-the-age-of-ai.md).
3. **Drafts the models themselves.** Generate draft domain models from natural language, produce instances that conform to a metamodel, recommend completions — with the human still owning the model and the output checked against the metamodel before it is trusted (source: 002-model-driven-development-in-the-age-of-ai.md).
4. **Shortens the feedback loop.** Executable models plus AI let you run or simulate behaviour at the model level and validate intent before committing to a platform (source: 002-model-driven-development-in-the-age-of-ai.md).

## The asymmetry of trust — the real point

The two translations want opposite tools (source: 002-model-driven-development-in-the-age-of-ai.md):

- **Upper translation (human need → formal model) is sense-making.** Here the LLM earns its place — as a collaborator that explains options, surfaces missed implications, proposes structure, argues back. Non-determinism is not a defect: nothing is trusted directly, a human reviews and signs off, and two runs giving two explanations is useful because the work is exploration. This is the part classic MDA did badly, and AI is exactly what makes it humane.
- **Lower translation (model → running code) is execution, and in a bank, government system or hospital it is where liability lives.** Here you want the property an LLM cannot give: the same model produces the same code every time, so an auditor can trace any line back to a decision in the model. A deterministic generator gives that; a probabilistic one does not. Recent work on LLMs in model-driven engineering found they tend to produce abstract, implicit constraints unfit for forward engineering without manual refinement. So in regulated domains you keep the LLM out of the runtime path (source: 002-model-driven-development-in-the-age-of-ai.md).

Splitting the layers this way *resolves* the trust problem rather than ducking it, and rehabilitates the half of classic MDA the essay had called a cost: the deterministic, portable transformation is not dead weight — in a regulated setting it is the auditable, reproducible asset. AI fixed classic MDA's brutal top of the funnel and left the trustworthy bottom intact. One caveat keeps the cost honest: you may use an LLM to draft, refactor and maintain that deterministic generator at authoring time under review and test — you just never let it *run* the transformation (source: 002-model-driven-development-in-the-age-of-ai.md).

## Where not to oversell it

- The abstraction-vs-control tension is moved, not repealed — when generated code is subtly wrong, someone must drop in and decide whether to fix the code or the model; if the honest answer is usually "fix the code", round-trip drift returns in new clothes (source: 002-model-driven-development-in-the-age-of-ai.md).
- Precision in natural language is itself a skill; English is ambiguous and that is what you cannot afford when a machine acts on words literally (source: 002-model-driven-development-in-the-age-of-ai.md).
- The comfortable cases stay comfortable — stable domains with recurring patterns gain most; genuinely novel work gains least, because there the bottleneck is deciding intent, which code-generation does not accelerate (source: 002-model-driven-development-in-the-age-of-ai.md).

## Key Takeaways

- The verdict is "yes, with conditions, and the conditions decide everything": the lightweight end (DSL or structured model + LLM transformation + metamodel contract) is genuinely valuable now and more broadly applicable than classic MDA ever was, because the two biggest costs — building transformations and building the language — both fell sharply (source: 002-model-driven-development-in-the-age-of-ai.md)
- The heavy end (UML/MOF/QVT) is rebalanced, not rescued: keep its deterministic, auditable descent for banking/government/healthcare; let AI carry the top-of-funnel load it always struggled with
- The division of labour is by layer: humans + AI own the *what*; the *how* is owned by whichever engine the stakes call for — an LLM where a wrong guess is cheap, a deterministic generator where it is not
- The deciding factor is cultural, not technical: teams that hold the line (maintain the model, regenerate the code, never quietly patch back to two sources of truth) get what MDD promised in 2001, finally at a sensible cost
